# Chapter 1. Probability and Uncertainty

<!-- cite:begin -->
From *Bayes, Artificial Intelligence, and Deep Learning: Foundations of Data Science* by Nick Polson and Vadim Sokolov (2026), chapter 1. Cite as Polson and Sokolov (2026), *Bayes, Artificial Intelligence, and Deep Learning: Foundations of Data Science*, edition 1. Book page: https://vsokolov.org/books/bayes_ai_deep_learning/. Source: https://github.com/VadimSokolov/bayes_ai_deep_learning.
<!-- cite:end -->

This chapter asks how uncertainty is measured and which rules probabilities must obey. It presents probability as a degree of belief revealed by betting odds, and argues through the Dutch book that coherence (no sure loss) implies the axioms of probability and the rule for conditional probability, derived in an appendix with payoff matrices and determinants. It then covers odds, random variables, mass, density and cumulative distribution functions, the inverse CDF method, change of variables, expectation, variance, covariance, correlation, portfolio variance, the weak law of large numbers (proved under finite variance with Chebyshev's inequality), the strong law and the ergodic theorem (stated), the Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson and normal distributions, joint, marginal and conditional distributions, and independence. Examples include exacta betting, the birthday problem, confounding in batting averages, and a normal model for the 1987 crash.

## From the literature

the betting definition of subjective probability (Ramsey 1926; de Finetti 1937); Dutch book coherence as the basis of the probability axioms (Ramsey 1926; de Finetti 1937; Shimony 1955); the axioms of probability (Kolmogorov 1933); the frequency definition through collectives (von Mises), criticized by Ville 1939; the weak law of large numbers (Jakob Bernoulli 1713, generalized by Poisson 1837 and Chebyshev 1867); the strong law for Bernoulli trials (Borel 1909); the strong law under a variance condition (Kolmogorov 1933); methods for studying coincidences, applied to the birthday problem (Diaconis and Mosteller 1989); the Harville formula for exacta bets (named in the chapter, no reference); the three coin paradox (Galton 1894, essay linked in the chapter); subjective expected utility (Savage, building on von Neumann and Morgenstern).
